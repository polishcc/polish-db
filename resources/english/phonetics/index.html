    Polish Phonetics

    The Polish Alphabet

    The Polish alphabet is based on the Latin alphabet, but it contains 32 letters. In addition to omitting a few letters that are not used in Polish (Q, V, X), it also includes several letters unique to the Polish language.

    The modern Polish alphabet is:

    Aa Ąą Bb Cc Ćć Dd Ee Ęę Ff Gg Hh Ii Jj Kk Ll Łł Mm Nn Ńń Oo Óó Pp Rr Ss Śś Tt Uu Ww Yy Zz Źź Żż

    The small hook beneath the letters ą and ę is called an ogonek (Polish for "little tail").

    Besides single letters, Polish also has several digraphs (two letters representing a single sound):

    ch, cz, dz, dź, dż, rz, sz

    The letters Q, V, and X are not part of the Polish alphabet. They appear only in certain abbreviations (for example, V for volt, which is wolt in Polish) or in a few words of foreign origin (such as taxi and quiz).

    Stress

    Unlike French, in Polish the stress falls on the penultimate (second-to-last) syllable in almost all cases.

    The main exceptions, where the stress falls on the third syllable from the end, are:

    • Words of Greek origin ending in -ika and -yka (fizyka [ˈfʲizɨka])
    • Past-tense verbs with the endings -śmy and -ście (pytaliście [pɨˈtalʲiɕʨɛ] instead of [pɨtaˈlʲiɕʨɛ])
    • Conditional verbs with the endings -bym, -byś and -by (robiłbym [ˈrɔbʲiwbɨm] instead of [rɔˈbʲiwbɨm])
    • Numerals (czterysta [ˈʧtɛrɨsta] instead of [ʧtɛˈrɨsta])
    • Numerals followed by the suffix -kroć (tysiąckroć [ˈtɨɕɔnʦkrɔʨ] instead of [tɨˈɕɔnʦkrɔʨ])

    There are also exceptions where the stress falls on the fourth syllable from the end:

    • Conditional verbs with the endings -byśmy and -byście (pisalibyśmy [pʲiˈsalʲibɨɕmɨ] instead of [pʲisalʲiˈbɨɕmɨ])
    • Past-tense verbs with the endings -żeśmy and -żeście (spytaliżeście [spɨˈtalʲiʒɛɕʨɛ] instead of [spɨtalʲiˈʒɛɕʨɛ])

    Pronunciation

    Vowels

    Polish vowels are always pronounced short.

    IPA Letter Written example Audio example English equivalent / approximation
    [a] a tak a as in father
    [ɛ] e krew e as in bed
    [i] i i ee as in see, but short
    [ɔ] o oko roughly the o in British English not
    [u] ó, u mówić, ku oo as in food, but short
    [ɨ] y syn No exact English equivalent. It is pronounced farther back than i, with the lips unrounded.
    [ɔ̃] ą mąż No direct English equivalent: a nasalized o
    [ɛ̃] ę język No direct English equivalent: a nasalized e

    When spelling a word aloud, the letters a, e, i, and o are simply named after their respective sounds.

    However, both ó and u are pronounced [u]. To distinguish them, they are called:

    • ó: u zamknięte ("closed u")
    • u: u otwarte ("open u")

    Other names for ó are also commonly used:

    • o kreskowane ("crossed o")
    • o z kreską ("o with a stroke")

    The choice between ó and u is largely etymological (based on the historical development of the word). As a result, confusing these two letters is one of the most common spelling mistakes in Polish.

    The letter y is called igrek [ˈigrɛk] (borrowed from the French i grec). Among all Polish vowels, y is the only one that can never appear at the beginning of a word.

    The consonants and the letter i

    In Polish, the letter i placed after a consonant palatalizes it (makes it “soft”).

    When a vowel follows i, the i serves only to palatalize the consonant.

    i before a vowel:

    • after the letters c, s, z, dz and n, it is not pronounced.
    • after other consonants, it is generally pronounced like [j].

    Thus, the palatal consonants ć, ś, ź, dź and ń are written:

    • ć, ś, ź, dź, ń before a consonant or at the end of a word;
    • ci, si, zi, dzi, ni before a vowel.
    Letters IPA Written example Audio example Notes
    bi [bʲ] biały
    pi [pʲ] piasek
    gi [gʲ] ogień
    ki [kʲ] cukier
    wi [wʲ] wiatr
    fi [fʲ] geografia
    di [dʲ] diabeł
    dzi [ʥ] dziad equivalent to dź
    ti [tʲ] sympatia
    ci [ʨ] ciasto equivalent to ć
    zi [ʑ] zielony equivalent to ź
    si [ɕ] siarka equivalent to ś
    hi [çʲ] hiena
    li [lʲ] afiliacja
    mi [mʲ] anatomia
    ni [ɲ] kuchnia equivalent to ń
    ria [rʲ] historia

    Note that soft consonants can never precede y, and hard consonants almost never precede i. (Exceptions: loanwords, for example dżinsy [ˈʤinsɨ] = jeans)

    Nasal vowels

    The letters ę and ą are nasal vowels: ę is a nasal e and ą is a nasal o. Their pronunciation depends heavily on the consonants that follow them.

    Letter Following consonant Written example IPA Audio example English equivalent
    ą f, w, h, ch, s, z, sz, ż, rz, ś, ź, end of word mąż [ɔ̃] No exact English equivalent; a nasalized o, similar to French on
    b, p skąpiec [ɔm] like om in Polish pompka
    g, k pociąg [ɔŋ] like on in Polish biedronka
    c, dz, d, t, cz, dż piątek [ɔn] on in Polish konto
    ć, dź bądź [ɔɲ] oń in Polish koń
    ę f, w, h, ch, s, z, sz, ż, rz, ś, ź język [ɛ̃]
    b, p gęba [ɛm] like em in Polish zemsta
    g, k dźwięk [ɛŋ] like en in Polish piosenka
    c, dz, d, t, cz, dż wędrować [ɛn] en in Polish ten
    ć, dź wszędzie [ɛɲ] eń in Polish pień
    end of word imię [ɛ] a regular Polish e

    “Simple” consonants

    We first present the consonants that also exist in English. At the end of a word or before a voiceless consonant, voiced consonants that have a voiceless counterpart become their voiceless equivalents.

    Letter Written example Audio example IPA English equivalent
    b bardzo [b] b in bed, b is never very plosive, the lips only touch
    chleb [p] p in pen, p is never very plosive, the lips only touch
    p pan
    d dom [d] d in dog, unlike in English, though, the tongue touches the teeth
    kod [t] t in top, unlike in English, though, the toungue touches the teeth
    t tak
    g góra [g] g in go
    katalog [k] k in key
    k kod
    w waga [v] v in very
    staw [f] f in fish
    f fala
    z za [z] z in zoo
    raz [s] s in see
    s sam
    j jajo [j] y in yes
    l lato [l] a clear/light l, with the tongue tip just behind the upper teeth and no raising of the back of the tongue
    m moment [m] m in man
    n noga [n] n in no
    ń koń [ɲ] similar to ny in canyon
    ł łuk [w] w in water

    Consonants with no exact equivalent in English

    We now present consonants that do not have an exact equivalent in English. Here too, at the end of a word or before a voiceless consonant, voiced consonants that have a voiceless counterpart become their voiceless equivalents.

    Letter Written example Audio example IPA Notes
    dz dzwon [dz] dz is close to ds in the English words adze or kids, but pronounced as a single movement.
    ksiądz [ts] c is close to ts in tsunami or to the German pronunciation of Zeit. It is the voiceless counterpart of dz.
    c cena
    ż, rz żaba, rzeka [ʒ] similar to s in English vision, but articulated farther back. The sound is darker and “thicker.”
    masaż, kalendarz [ʂ] similar to sh in English ship, but harder and articulated farther back in the mouth
    sz szef
    dż dżem [d͡ʒ] similar to j in English jam, but articulated slightly farther back
    cz czas [t͡ʂ]] similar to ch in English chair, but with the tongue slightly farther back in the mouth
    ź źle [ʑ] ź has no exact English equivalent; it is similar to s in vision, but softer and pronounced farther forward in the mouth.
    znaleźć [ɕ] ś is the voiceless counterpart of ź. It is similar to sh in English sheep, but softer and pronounced farther forward.
    ś świat
    dź dźwig [ʥ] dź is similar to j in English jeans, but softer and pronounced farther forward.
    miedź [ʨ] ć is the voiceless counterpart of dź. It is similar to ch in English cheese, but softer and pronounced farther forward in the mouth.
    ć ćma
    h, ch charakter, hotel [x] h and ch are pronounced the same way. English has no exact equivalent; the sound is similar to ch in Scottish loch or German Bach. It is produced as a stream of air at the back of the mouth, without vibrating the throat.
    r rdza [r] r is pronounced by vibrating the tip of the tongue just behind the upper teeth
